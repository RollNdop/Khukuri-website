Heritage

The khukuri: origin and heritage

A blade with a longer story than most tools ever get.

Where it comes from

The exact origin of the khukuri is debated by historians. Some trace its curved shape to older blade forms that reached the region long ago; others tie its modern form to the Nepali kingdoms of several centuries back. What's agreed is that it has been a fixture of Nepali life — as both tool and weapon — for a very long time.

The Gurkha khukuri

The khukuri's global reputation comes largely from its long association with Gurkha soldiers, who have served in British and Indian regiments since the early 1800s. It became a signature part of Gurkha equipment, and its reputation only grew through both World Wars.

A common myth, gently corrected

Many people have heard that a khukuri "must draw blood once drawn." This is folklore, not a literal rule — a good story, but not something to repeat as fact. Being upfront about this kind of detail tends to build more trust with knowledgeable buyers than repeating the legend uncritically.

More than a blade

In Nepal, the khukuri is also a working tool and a household object — used for everything from clearing brush to kitchen tasks — and it holds a place in cultural and festival traditions as a symbol of protection and strength.

Look closely near the handle of a khukuri and you'll usually find a small notch cut into the blade, called the kaudi. Explanations for it vary by region and storyteller, but it's one of the most recognisable and asked-about details on the entire knife.
